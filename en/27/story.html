The Comeback Story

The comeback of Choji Murata, told in 5 chapters

01

Origin1949–1982

An Island Boy and the Masakari Pitching Form

Toyoda District, Hiroshima Prefecture. Choji Murata grew up on an island in the Seto Inland Sea, watching the back of his fisherman father. From a father who knew the harshness of the sea, he learned perseverance and grit.

After graduating high school, he joined the Lotte Orions. He rose to prominence with his distinctive "Masakari" (battle-axe) pitching form. With an enormous windup and his entire body coiling into the delivery, his motion was likened to the swing of a woodsman's axe splitting a log.

A fastball approaching 150 km/h and a devastating forkball. Murata anchored the pitching staff as the ace and grew into one of professional baseball's marquee hurlers. The boy from a remote island was shining on the mound.

02

Despair1982

A Shattered Right Elbow -- In an Era When "Surgery Means Retirement"

In 1982, a catastrophic injury struck his right elbow. The elbow -- a pitcher's very lifeblood -- was destroyed. In the Japanese baseball world of that era, the prevailing wisdom was blunt: "Have elbow surgery and you'll never throw again." The overwhelming majority advised retirement.

"They told me surgery would be the end."

He was 33. He still wanted to pitch. He still wanted to stand on the mound. But surgery meant retirement -- that wall was the limit of medicine itself at the time. The end of his career loomed right before his eyes.

His wife Yoshiko watched over her husband's agony and quietly stayed by his side. "I will support whatever you decide" -- those words alone anchored Murata through his indecision. The right arm that had electrified Japanese baseball with the Masakari form might never throw again. Murata had no choice but to face that reality.

03

The Void1983–1984

Crossing the Pacific -- Entrusting His Elbow to Dr. Jobe's Scalpel

In 1983, Murata decided to go to America. He would undergo Tommy John surgery under Dr. Frank Jobe in Los Angeles. For a Japanese professional baseball player, it was a pioneering decision.

At the time, the procedure was virtually unknown in Japan, and its success rate was far from certain. "Then I'll make new history" -- Murata's words carried both resolve and defiance.

The two years following surgery were an unbroken chain of solitary rehabilitation. On American soil, he threw day after day in silent isolation. Letters arrived from Japan, but the feel of the mound grew ever more distant. "Will I really be able to pitch again?" -- the battle against that doubt was the hardest of all.

Letters and phone calls from his wife Yoshiko warmed his solitude across the ocean. "You can do it" -- trusting in her voice, Murata threw 100 pitches every day. He threw until his arm could no longer rise, and the next morning he threw again. The fisherman's endurance cultivated on that island sustained him through every day of rehabilitation.

04

Awakening1985

Sunday Choji -- The Sunday Miracle

In 1985, Choji Murata was reborn. And his return was no ordinary comeback. He kept winning in his Sunday starts, and the nickname "Sunday Choji" was born.

That year he posted 17 victories in a spectacular resurgence. He overpowered hitters with the same velocity as before his surgery -- no, with even greater velocity. The Masakari form was alive and well.

"Life is a complete game. I want to stay on my own two feet until the very end."

Every Sunday, the stadium filled with fans who had come to watch Murata pitch. "Sunday Choji" was not merely a nickname; it became a weekly ritual that gave hope to the entire nation.

The man they said would retire if he had surgery posted the finest season of his career after surgery. It was a miracle that overturned conventional wisdom, and it blazed a trail for every pitcher who would follow.

05

Rebirth1985–2022

Pioneer of Japanese Baseball -- The Eternal Masakari Form

Two hundred fifteen career victories. Induction into the Meikyukai (the Hall of Fame for 200-game winners). Choji Murata etched his name into the annals of Japanese professional baseball. Yet his greatest legacy was not his win total but the precedent he set: that a pitcher could return to the pros after Tommy John surgery.

"Because of his comeback, modern pitchers can pursue their return without fearing surgery." (Baseball commentator)

Shohei Ohtani and countless other contemporary pitchers have come back from Tommy John surgery. The man who walked that road first was Choji Murata. The courage to cross the Pacific and entrust his right elbow to Dr. Jobe's scalpel changed the future of Japanese baseball.

A fisherman's son from a remote island electrified the nation with his Masakari form, overturned surgical dogma, and expanded what was possible for pitchers. Choji Murata's story is the epic complete game of a man who turned an "ending" into a "beginning."

In 2022, Murata passed away. But the path he pioneered stretches on beneath the feet of countless pitchers to this day.
